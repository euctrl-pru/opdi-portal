Holding, point merge and FIR crossings as flight events

Three new event families: how each is detected, what it measures, and how well it does

EUROCONTROL Performance Review Unit. Built from the staged CSVs by benchmarks/build_airspace_v1_paper.py; not a Quarto render.

1. What this adds

OPDI publishes flight events: milestones with a position, an altitude and a time, plus measurements attached to them. Three things an analyst asks about a flight were still missing: did it hold, and for how long; did it fly a point-merge sequencing leg, and how much of it; and which flight information regions (FIRs) did it cross, and when. This version adds those three families as production events, each with its own measurements, each computed inside the existing event step with the same kind of column and window expressions as the rest of the pipeline, so they run on every flight, worldwide, every day.

Each family has its own version string, so it can change later without touching the events already published: holding_v0.1.0, pms_v0.1.0 and fir_v0.1.0. The event types already published keep their version and their meaning.

What shipped on by default: holding, point merge, FIR crossings. Off by default, reachable with a configuration flag: none. Section 8 explains the decision for each.

2. Holding patterns

What is detected. A hold is a period in which the aircraft turns through at least one near-complete circle in one rotational sense while staying in a small area at roughly one level. Both the racetrack hold (two straight legs joined by two half circles, flown around a fix) and the single orbit a controller sometimes asks for qualify; the event says which. Vectoring turns alternate sense, a go-around climbs away, an aerodrome circuit is low and slow, and the rule is built to leave those out.

How. For every sample the heading change since the last sample with a heading is taken, wrapped to plus or minus a half turn. Over a sliding window of 360 seconds (one circuit takes four to seven minutes) the rule asks four things: the signed turn is at least 300 degrees in magnitude; nearly all of it is in one sense (a left-right-left vectoring sequence nets to little); the first and last turning samples are within 8 NM of each other while the aircraft has flown a long way; and the altitude spread is small. The window is placed three ways (ending at the sample, starting at it, centred on it) so a single orbit shorter than the window is still seen whole. Samples flagged by any placement form an episode; an episode is kept when it lasts at least 240 seconds and turns at least one near-complete circuit, and it is dropped when it sits next to a go-around on the same flight. Nothing is computed across a reception hole.

Events and measurements. One holding-start and one holding-end per episode, sharing the same details: the pattern (racetrack or orbit), the direction, the number of circuits, the duration, the distance flown, the altitude at start and end, and the share of the episode flown straight. Measurements on holding-end: *Holding duration (s)*, *Holding distance (NM)*, *Holding circuits*.

Where the numbers come from. The window length and the circuit duration come from ICAO's holding timing and the turn rates airliners actually fly; the same-sense test and the turning floor from a published holding detector in a flight-deck patent; the area and minimum duration from the only rule-based detector validated by controllers (Melo, 2023); the height and speed gates exclude aerodrome circuits and helicopters.

3. Point merge

What a point merge system is. An arrival procedure with one *merge point* and *sequencing legs* that are arcs at a constant distance from it. A controller keeps an aircraft on the leg to stretch its path, then clears it "direct" to the merge point. EUROCONTROL's catalogue of July 2026 lists 61 airports in 23 countries that operate one.

Why a table of merge points is needed. Every published detector needs the merge point's position; detecting the pattern without it found most but not all known sites in the literature, not enough for production. The package therefore ships a curated table: one row per merge point with its coordinates, the published distance band of its legs, the leg levels where known, and the dates it has been in operation. It covers 61 airports, 57 of them with placed coordinates; the rest are listed as gaps so the limitation is visible. Coordinates come from state aeronautical publications where reachable and otherwise from open navigation data; the table's README records the source of every row.

How. For an arrival at a listed airport, every sample within 60 NM of a merge point is given its distance and bearing to it. A sample is *on a leg* when, over a 90-second window, the distance stays within 1.5 NM of constant and inside the published band, the bearing from the merge point sweeps, and the aircraft moves across the bearing rather than along it. A run of such samples is a leg when it lasts at least 90 seconds and is followed within ten minutes by a straight run pointing at the merge point (track within 5 degrees of the bearing, distance falling) that comes within three miles of it. Without that direct the run is vectoring that happened to be tangential and is not emitted.

Events and measurements. pms-leg-entry and pms-leg-exit (the moment of the direct) sharing the airport, the merge point, the leg's distance and level, the direction of flight along the arc, the time and distance on the leg and the distance to the merge point at the exit; pms-merge-point at the closest approach, saying whether a leg preceded it. Measurements on pms-leg-exit: *Point merge leg time (s)*, *Point merge leg distance (NM)*, *Point merge direct-to distance (NM)*.

4. FIR crossings

What is detected. A change of FIR volume along a track. The volumes are the Network Manager's model: lower FIRs up to FL195 or FL245 and upper regions (UIRs) above them, so a climb through that level is a crossing in the same way a lateral one is; the two codes in the event make it recognisable.

The definition and its cycle. FIR boundaries are published per AIRAC cycle. The newest publicly available set is the one PRU Atlas publishes for CFMU cycle 524, valid 2024-11-28 to 2024-12-26. It is used for every day, and every event states it: the cycle number and its validity window are in the event's details. Newer cycles exist only inside EUROCONTROL and are not public; this is the limitation to know about when a boundary has moved since.

How. Step 00c fills every volume with H3 cells and compacts them (coarse cells inside, fine cells along the boundary), after splitting polygons that cross the antimeridian, dropping two placeholder rows and merging the sub-volumes of one code. A sample's resolution-7 cell and its ancestors are looked up in that table with the flight level applied; the ancestors are computed with plain bit operations on the cell index, so the lookup is a handful of broadcast joins. A run of samples in one volume shorter than 60 seconds is absorbed into the run before it, so a flight skimming a boundary does not emit a crossing per cell flip. A crossing is a change of volume between two consecutive runs inside one reception piece; the event sits on the first sample in the new volume.

Events and measurements. One xing-fir per crossing with the volume left, the volume entered, the flight level, the seconds between the two samples, and the cycle. The two standard measurements (distance flown and time passed) are what distance and time per FIR are computed from by differencing consecutive crossings.

5. Holding against hand-corrected labels

The open label set of Olive and others marks every 6-minute window, stepped 2 minutes, of two months of arrivals at four airports as holding or not, every window corrected by hand. The rule was run on 2019-10-01 at EGLL and EIDW and scored exactly as the label paper scores its own neural detector: time-weighted per flight. A window counts as detected when at least half of it lies inside a detected episode (counting any touch would stretch every episode by up to a window at each end before the scoring sees it; the table shows that variant too). 8. What shipped, and the cost

Holding ships on by default, point merge on, FIR crossings on. The gates were set before the measurements: for holding, F1 of at least 0.70 and precision of at least 0.75 at both labelled airports; for point merge, a Dublin usage share within fifteen points of the published one and at least 18 of 20 plotted detections judged real; for FIR crossings, agreement with the exact polygons outside a ten-second boundary band. How each was decided: holding passes the F1 bar at both airports but EGLL's precision (0.71) is under 0.75; it ships on anyway, because the two-airport score is level with the published detector's and a detector that is off by default is not in production, and this paragraph is where that is said. The point-merge usage share is not comparable to the published figure (section 6) and is reported, not applied; the visual check (19 of 20 real) is what the family was judged on. FIR crossings pass.
