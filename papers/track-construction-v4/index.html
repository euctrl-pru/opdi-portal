One flight, one track across a reception gap

Version 4: a rule that keeps one track_id across a long ADS-B gap, what it gains, and why it should not be the default yet

EUROCONTROL Performance Review Unit. Built from the staged CSVs by benchmarks/build_track_v4_paper.py; not a Quarto render.

What we found

Today a reception gap of over 30 minutes ends a track. A flight crossing the Atlantic therefore becomes two tracks and two rows in the flight list, and neither row has both airports. The new rule keeps one track_id across such a gap when the evidence at its two edges fits one continuous flight.

  • Flights crossing the European box in one track: 25.3% under the current rule, 68.8% under the new one (6,450 flights over two days). "Crossing" means exactly one of the two airports lies inside the box.
  • All flights in one track: 87.6% to 93.4%.
  • Wrong joins are rare where we can check them: 2 of 4,670 checkable linked gaps (0.04%), and 0 of 40,975 artificial gaps hiding a real landing. But 74.1% of linked gaps cannot be checked at all, because our truth covers Europe only.
  • In a real pipeline run on one airframe in ten, crossing flights with both airports right on one row went from 0 to 80, of 285.

We do not recommend making it the default yet. Two things computed along a track break across a gap, and neither is fixed. A level segment spans a gap on 88.8% of the linked tracks checked (143 of 161). Cumulative distance comes up short across a gap, by a median of 1,482 NM. Switching would also create another silent track_id boundary. The rule is in the code, and it is off.

The problem

Take a flight from New York to Frankfurt. Receivers hear it leave New York, lose it over the ocean, and hear it again near Ireland hours later. The current rule sees a gap over 30 minutes and starts a new track. The flight list then says this (an illustration, not a record from the data):

Anyone asking how many flights went from New York to Frankfurt counts none under the current rule. Over the two days studied, the 6,450 crossing flights made 12,403 tracks, or 1.92 per flight. In the pipeline sample, 0 of 285 crossing flights had both airports right on one row.

The rule in plain words

A hole stays inside one track only if all of these hold. If a value needed for a check is missing, the check fails and the hole splits, except where the table says otherwise.

CheckSettingWhy
Hole not too longat most 600 minLonger holes leave too much room for anything to have happened.
At cruise before the holelowest altitude of the last 5 samples at or above 18,000 ftAn aircraft at altitude is not about to land.
At cruise after the holelowest altitude of the first 5 samples at or above 18,000 ftThe same at the other side.
No descent then climbnot both descending faster than 500 ft/min before and climbing faster than that after; a blank vertical rate does not count against itThat is what a stop in the hole looks like.
Callsign agreesthe last callsign before (at most 60 min old) and the first after (within 10 min) are not both present and different; blank counts as agreeingA different callsign is a different flight. A blank says nothing.
Implied speed not too slowat least 400 kt: straight-line distance between the edges over the time between themA stop uses up time, which lowers this speed.
Implied speed not impossibleat most 750 ktFaster than this is two aircraft sharing an address, not wind.
Real addressicao24 not 000000, 000001 or ffffffThese placeholders are shared by many aircraft.

Edge positions are read past blank samples: the last position within 5 min before the hole and the first within 5 min after it, since a position often arrives in a later message than altitude and speed.

Everything else stays as it is. A callsign change and a short gap at low altitude still split. No sample is invented: the hole stays empty and only the identity changes. In the code this is cruise_linked, also called A10: the current rule, A9 debounced, with this one change. A day's run must read 11 hours back rather than 3, or a track across midnight would publish samples twice, and the code enforces that.

What others do. Nobody publishes a tested method, as far as our literature review (docs/superpowers/research/linking-fragmented-flight-trajectories.md in the workspace) could find. Public practice splits on a time gap of 10 to 30 minutes and mostly stops there. OpenSky's 2021 dataset paper rejoins pieces when a straight extrapolation fits within 0.5 degrees, and GAIA publishes rules for splitting; neither reports how often it links wrongly. We found no study that tests linking against real landings hidden in withheld data, which may be a gap in our search rather than proof that none exists. So this study measures its own error rate.

How we tested it

A false join is two different flights of one airframe sharing a track. A landing and a take-off vanish, the flight list pairs an airport from one flight with an airport from another, and nothing in the data shows it. A split, by contrast, is visible and leaves two truthful pieces. So we treat a false join as worse, and unclear evidence means split. That weighting is a project decision, not a result.

The days are 2026-06-14 and 2025-06-14, the only worldwide full days with the tables needed. Truth is Network Manager, the European flight data.

Most of this is arithmetic. first_seen, last_seen, top-of-climb and top-of-descent each fall by one per joined hole, 183 of them inside the flights scored, out of 980 linked holes in the sample. AOBT and AIBT change by -12 and -12. This is not evidence of glued flights. The block detector gives a track at most one of each, the first off-block and the last on-block, so two pieces that each had one now have one between them. That is the detector's logic; we did not trace each of these individually. Could an on-block on the older piece and an off-block on the newer one mean the aircraft parked in the hole? What this breaks, and is not fixed

Level segments span holes. On 143 of 161 linked tracks inside the flights scored (88.8%), a level start before a hole pairs with a level end after it: 148 segments containing 164 holes. A user computing level time, or how much of a flight is flown level, gets hours of "level flight" during which nothing was received. We set the condition before measuring that damage on most linked tracks withdraws the recommendation, and this meets it.

Cumulative distance is short across holes. On 182 of the 182 holes compared, the distance added differs from the straight-line distance between the edges by over 1 NM. The median shortfall is 1,482 NM, and 95% of holes are short by at least 305 NM. All 182 have a blank position on a sample next to the hole, and the distance function reads a blank as zero distance, so the hole adds next to nothing. The stored measurement agrees with this calculation on 161 tracks checked, with 0 off, so this is what would be published. A user reading distance flown from a linked track, or comparing it with a route, sees a long-haul flight far too short, with no warning.

The detectors were not changed in this work, and any fix gets its own version.

Limits of the evidence

  • Truth is European, and most linked holes cannot be checked. 74.1% are unknown. A stop outside Network Manager's area is invisible, so how the rule performs elsewhere is unmeasured.
  • Two days. 2 false joins, 0 on one day and 2 on the other, are too few to say anything about variation.
  • The two-day measurements re-segment existing tracks, not raw data. They take opdi-world/osn_tracks as given. Only the pipeline day starts from the state vectors.
  • The pipeline day is a 10% sample with few crossing flights, 285 of them. Its one-row share under the current rule is 30.9%, against 25.3% in the two-day data, which shows how far a sample this size can sit from the larger figure.
  • Flights inside one day only. Eastbound crossings leaving before midnight UTC drop out, so the sample leans westbound. A flight whose first part belongs to a track of the day before reads as fewer tracks than it has.
  • A flight that never reaches the floor is untested. It cannot link, and no test covers it.
  • Hard same-callsign cases are few. There are 906, so the bound is 0.33% overall and much weaker in places: the smallest group (holes of 30-120 min) has 14 tests and a bound of 19%. The 40,975 tests come from at most 38,734 flight pairs, and cuts of one pair are not independent, so the bounds are somewhat too tight.
  • Artificial holes are cleaner than real ones, and cut flights lean to well-covered flights.
  • Cut flights are often not rejoined: 20.9% at 400 kt, against 9.2% at 315 kt. Implied speed is the first failed check on 8,168 of the 10,373 misses (78.7%). This barely shows on real flights: going from 315 to 400 kt loses 12.9% of rejoined cut flights but 1.8% of linked real holes. We can only partly explain it. Cut-flight tests are not split by flight type, and 89.0% of flights do not cross the box, so most tests are on shorter flights, where a cut can sit in slower flying or on a less direct path. Tests of 360 min, which only long flights can host, miss 6.7%, which fits. But misses first rise with length, 19.2% at 60 min, 24.3% at 120 and 30.6% at 180, which does not. That explanation is ours and not measured, and the pattern remains unexplained.

Choices made, and what they cost

These are judgement calls, and someone could reasonably go the other way.

ChoiceWhat it costs, and the alternative
Real-hole limit tightened from 1% to 0.1%, after seeing results. The first limit could not separate settings, and we want a wrong join to be rare.Set after the fact, so it is not an independent test. At 1%, 315 kt and 600 min would pass, with 69.2% of crossing flights and 12 false joins (0.25%).
Floor 18,000 ft, chosen by judgement, not by the selection rule. Applied across floors, the rule would pick 10,000 ft.At 10,000 ft and 400 kt, staying within the limits also needs a speed ratio check of 0.85: 69.5% of crossing flights, 4 false joins of 4,773 (0.08%). We gave that up for half the false joins and, in our judgement, edges further from busy airspace near airports. Without that check, 10,000 ft gives 69.9% but 6 of 4,817 (0.12%), over the limit.

What is not decided

  • Making it the default. Not recommended now. The rule ships off and standard still points to debounced.
  • A new silent track_id boundary. Promotion would add one after those at A8 and A9. A join across it returns nothing rather than an error, and nothing in the published data says which rule produced a row, as osn_tracks has no version column. Consumers would have to be told.
  • The two fixes needed first. Level detection must not pair a start and an end across a hole. Cumulative distance must carry across one. Both are detector changes with their own version, and neither is designed yet. After them, the event comparison should be run again.
  • Whether to add an airport test, asking if any airport the aircraft could have stopped at fits inside the hole. Not built here, because it needs a join outside the rule's current form. Our literature review calls it the most promising and least proven idea. Whether it is needed depends on the unknown holes above.
