One flight, one track across a reception gap

Version 4: a rule that keeps one track_id across a long ADS-B gap, what it gains, and why it should not be the default yet

EUROCONTROL Performance Review Unit. Built from the staged CSVs by benchmarks/build_track_v4_paper.py; not a Quarto render.

Superseded by the next version. This version proposed the rule and measured it, and recommended against making it the default until two detectors were fixed. The next version fixes them, audits the rule, re-measures on the final code and makes it the default. The numbers below are those this version published; its pipeline-day figures for the new rule were later found to rest on a stale candidate table, and the next version replaces them. This page is frozen: rendering it no longer re-runs the analysis.

What we found

Today a reception gap of over 30 minutes ends a track. A flight crossing the Atlantic therefore becomes two tracks and two rows in the flight list, and neither row has both airports. The new rule keeps one track_id across such a gap when the evidence at its two edges fits one continuous flight.

  • Flights crossing the European box in one track: 25.3% under the current rule, 80.7% under the new one (6,450 flights over two days). "Crossing" means exactly one of the two airports lies inside the box.
  • All flights in one track: 87.6% to 95.0%.
  • Two things took crossing flights from 68.8% to 80.7%. Reading altitude, vertical rate and speed at a gap's edge past blank samples gives 79.1%. Letting a flight lost while still climbing link gives 80.7%. A flight that reappears descending stays split, because that case failed its own limit.
  • Wrong joins are rare where we can check them: 3 of 5,773 checkable linked gaps (0.05%), and 6 of 123,949 artificial gaps hiding a real landing. But 74.9% of linked gaps cannot be checked at all, because our truth covers Europe only.
  • The climb part is provisional. Gaps linked only because the flight was climbing show 0 false joins in 185 checkable cases, under the 300 we wanted. All 6 artificial gaps that joined have a climbing edge and the same or a blank callsign.
  • In a real pipeline run on one airframe in ten, crossing flights with both airports right on one row went from 0 to 105, of 285.

We do not recommend making it the default yet. Two things computed along a track break across a gap, and neither is fixed. A level segment spans a gap on 88.0% of the linked tracks checked (169 of 192). Cumulative distance comes up short across a gap, by a median of 1,508 NM. Switching would also create another silent track_id boundary. The rule is in the code, and it is not the default. Its settings are provisional for climbing edges.

The problem

Take a flight from New York to Frankfurt. Receivers hear it leave New York, lose it over the ocean, and hear it again near Ireland hours later. The current rule sees a gap over 30 minutes and starts a new track. The flight list then says this (an illustration, not a record from the data):

Anyone asking how many flights went from New York to Frankfurt counts none under the current rule. Over the two days studied, the 6,450 crossing flights made 12,403 tracks, or 1.92 per flight. In the pipeline sample, 0 of 285 crossing flights had both airports right on one row.

The rule in plain words

A hole stays inside one track only if all of these hold. If a value needed for a check is missing, the check fails and the hole splits, except where the table says otherwise.

CheckSettingWhy
Hole not too longat most 600 minLonger holes leave too much room for anything to have happened.
At cruise or climbing before the holelowest of the nearest 5 altitudes within 5 min before the hole at or above 18,000 ft; or, below that, climbing: the mean of the nearest 5 vertical rates above 500 ft/min, resting on at least 3 ratesAn aircraft at altitude is not about to land, and one still climbing has just left. A blank rate does not count as climbing.
At cruise after the holelowest of the nearest 5 altitudes within 5 min after the hole at or above 18,000 ftThe same at the other side. A descending aircraft below the floor does not pass: see Results.
No descent then climbnot both descending faster than 500 ft/min before and climbing faster than that after; a blank vertical rate does not count against itThat is what a stop in the hole looks like.
Callsign agreesthe last callsign before (at most 60 min old) and the first after (within 10 min) are not both present and different; blank counts as agreeingA different callsign is a different flight. A blank says nothing.
Implied speed not too slowat least 400 kt: straight-line distance between the edge positions over the time between themA stop uses up time, which lowers this speed.
Implied speed not impossibleat most 750 ktFaster than this is two aircraft sharing an address, not wind.
Real addressicao24 not 000000, 000001 or ffffffThese placeholders are shared by many aircraft.

Every edge value is read past blank samples, within 5 min of the hole. ADS-B sends position and altitude separately from speed and vertical rate, so the sample right at an edge often lacks one or the other. So position uses the last value within 5 min before the hole and the first within 5 min after it. Altitude, vertical rate and speed each use their own nearest 5 values within the same 5 min. With no altitude in that span the check fails. With no vertical rate, the descent-then-climb check passes, and the climbing alternative does not.

Everything else stays as it is. A callsign change and a short gap at low altitude still split. No sample is invented: the hole stays empty and only the identity changes. In the code this is cruise_linked, also called A10: the current rule, A9 debounced, with this one change. A day's run must read 11 hours back rather than 3, or a track across midnight would publish samples twice, and the code enforces that.

What others do. Nobody publishes a tested method, as far as our literature review, "Link flight pieces only where stops cannot fit", could find. Public practice splits on a time gap of 10 to 30 minutes and mostly stops there. OpenSky's 2021 dataset paper rejoins pieces when a straight extrapolation fits within 0.5 degrees, and GAIA publishes rules for splitting; neither reports how often it links wrongly. We found no study that tests linking against real landings hidden in withheld data, which may be a gap in our search rather than proof that none exists. So this study measures its own error rate.

How we tested it

A false join is two different flights of one airframe sharing a track. A landing and a take-off vanish, the flight list pairs an airport from one flight with an airport from another, and nothing in the data shows it. A split, by contrast, is visible and leaves two truthful pieces. So we treat a false join as worse, and unclear evidence means split. That weighting is a project decision, not a result.

The days are 2026-06-14 and 2025-06-14, the only worldwide full days with the tables needed. Truth is Network Manager, the European flight data.

A setting had to meet three limits, pooled over the two days: false joins at most 0.1% of checkable real holes; at most 1% of hidden landings joined, read as a 95% upper bound; and at most 5% where the two flights have the same or a blank callsign, the case the callsign check cannot reject. Among settings within those limits, we took the most crossing flights in one track.

An edge allowed below the floor also has to pass the same three on its own, so a good result elsewhere cannot hide a bad one: at most 1% of the hidden landings of that kind, at most 5% of those with the same or a blank callsign, and at most 0.1% false joins among checkable holes linked only that way. A rate over a limit fails however small the sample. A rate within it, on under 300 checkable holes, passes only provisionally.

Climbing and descending are separate settings because of these same two days. We split them after a read of the same data showed one clean and one not. So the limits confirm a pattern already seen in that data. They are not a test of a prediction.

These two results point in opposite directions, and each could be chance. They do not show that the two kinds of edge differ. On real holes, climbing has 0 false joins of 185 and descending 3 of 119 (2.5%). If those 3 fell at random among the 304 checkable holes, the chance of all of them landing on the descending side is 0.059. On hidden landings it is the reverse: 6 of 82,974 joined with a climbing edge and 0 of 79,343 with a descending one. If those 6 fell at random among the 162,317 tests, the chance of all of them landing on the climbing side is 0.018. Timing may also play a part: 2 of the 3 descent false joins have the landing or take-off within 5 min of an edge, where an error in the truth time could produce a false join by itself. In the chosen rule, 0 of the 3 are that close. We have not checked those cases one by one.

Climbing on is a judgement. It gains 1.6 points of crossing flights and adds a way for a hidden landing to get through. At this sample size, "within the limit" means only that no false join was observed: 0 of 185 still allows up to 1.6%, at 95% confidence.

Descending off applies the limit. It has not been shown worse. It rests on 3 of 119. With so few checkable holes, a single false join would already read as 0.84%, over the limit, so one or two cases decide it. Turning it on as well would give 81.5% of crossing flights, 53 flights more, with 6 false joins of 5,893 (0.102%) overall.

What this breaks, and is not fixed

Level segments span holes. On 169 of 192 linked tracks inside the flights scored (88.0%), a level start before a hole pairs with a level end after it: 176 segments containing 206 holes. A user computing level time, or how much of a flight is flown level, gets hours of "level flight" during which nothing was received. We set the condition before measuring that damage on most linked tracks withdraws the recommendation, and this meets it.

Cumulative distance is short across holes. On 231 of the 231 holes compared, the distance added differs from the straight-line distance between the edges by over 1 NM. The median shortfall is 1,508 NM, and 95% of holes are short by at least 310 NM. All 231 have a blank position on a sample next to the hole, and the distance function reads a blank as zero distance, so the hole adds next to nothing. The stored measurement agrees with this calculation on 192 tracks checked, with 0 off, so this is what would be published. A user reading distance flown from a linked track, or comparing it with a route, sees a long-haul flight far too short, with no warning.

The detectors were not changed in this work, and any fix gets its own version.

Limits of the evidence

  • Truth is European, and most linked holes cannot be checked. 74.9% are unknown. A stop outside Network Manager's area is invisible, so how the rule performs elsewhere is unmeasured.
  • Two days. 3 false joins, 1 on one day and 2 on the other, are too few to say anything about variation.
  • The two-day measurements re-segment existing tracks, not raw data. They take opdi-world/osn_tracks as given. Only the pipeline day starts from the state vectors.
  • The pipeline day is a 10% sample with few crossing flights, 285 of them. Its one-row share under the current rule is 30.2%, against 25.3% in the two-day data, which shows how far a sample this size can sit from the larger figure.
  • Flights inside one day only. Eastbound crossings leaving before midnight UTC drop out, so the sample leans westbound. A flight whose first part belongs to a track of the day before reads as fewer tracks than it has.
  • A flight that never reaches the floor is untested. It cannot link, and no test covers it.
  • Climb evidence is thin. 185 checkable holes, under the 300 wanted, and 83.2% of holes linked this way cannot be checked. The 6 hidden landings it let through all fall in the 2,914 tests with a climbing edge that the callsign check cannot reject. No cut-flight test measures climbing alone: the low-edge cut removes a flight's whole cruise, so it needs climbing and descending together, and rejoins 0 of 35,804 under the chosen rule against 26,377 with both on.
  • The hidden-landing test does not cover a low climbing edge. It cuts at a flight's last climbing sample below the floor, so just under 18,000 ft, whereas the rule accepts an old edge at any altitude while it climbs. So its 6 of 82,974 covers the high-climb case only, and a low climbing edge is covered only by the 185 real holes. A departure lost low that lands outside Network Manager's area reads as unknown, and most climbing links are unknown (83.2%). There the 400 kt speed test does most of the protecting. That is our reading, not a measurement.
  • A reading can be up to 5 min old. An edge's altitude may come from a sample 5 min from the hole, so an aircraft could really be below the floor at the edge itself. 12,370 linked holes (53.8%) read the new edge's altitude past a blank, with 1 false join among them, and 46 (0.2%) read the old edge's, with 0. These count every hole with no altitude in the edge's own second, not only those the fix newly lets through. We have no measure of how far the aircraft moved in those minutes.
  • Hard same-callsign cases are few. There are 3,820, so the bound is 0.31% overall and much weaker in places: the smallest group (a climbing edge, holes of 120-240 min, cut 120 min into cruise) has 11 tests and a bound of 24%. The 123,949 tests come from at most 38,734 flight pairs, and cuts of one pair are not independent, so the bounds are somewhat too tight.
  • Artificial holes are cleaner than real ones, and cut flights lean to well-covered flights.
  • Cut flights are often not rejoined: 20.4% at 400 kt, against 8.5% at 315 kt. Implied speed is the first failed check on 8,293 of the 10,109 misses (82.0%). This barely shows on real flights: going from 315 to 400 kt loses 13.0% of rejoined cut flights but 1.8% of linked real holes. We can only partly explain it. Cut-flight tests are not split by flight type, and 89.0% of flights do not cross the box, so most tests are on shorter flights, where a cut can sit in slower flying or on a less direct path. Tests of 360 min, which only long flights can host, miss 6.4%, which fits. But misses first rise with length, 18.8% at 60 min, 23.6% at 120 and 29.6% at 180, which does not. That explanation is ours and not measured, and the pattern remains unexplained.

Choices made, and what they cost

These are judgement calls, and someone could reasonably go the other way.

A larger allowance is looser.At 35 min, crossing flights fall to 42.1%, and at 20 min to 32.7%. The safety gain is real: false joins 3 to 1 and hidden landings 6 to 0. The coverage loss is far larger, which is why it stays off.
Speed ratio check off. It compares implied speed with the aircraft's own speed at the edge.At 0.85, crossing flights 80.1% and false joins 2, against 80.7% and 3. A small trade either way, and we kept the simpler rule.
Climbing edges on, on evidence short of what we wanted.Gains 1.6 points of crossing flights, and adds a way for a hidden landing to get through: 6 of 82,974. Off gives 79.1% and 0 of 40,975 joined.
Descending edges off.Gives up 81.5% for 80.7% of crossing flights. They fail their own limit, on a small sample. See Results.
A blank callsign counted with same callsign in the hard case.868 of 3,820 tests (22.7%) are blank. The check cannot reject them, so this is the cautious way, but the group is not purely same-callsign flights.
Any landing or take-off in a hole counts as a false join.Overstates false joins, if anything.

What is not decided

  • Making it the default. Not recommended now. The rule ships off and standard still points to debounced.
  • A new silent track_id boundary. Promotion would add one after those at A8 and A9. A join across it returns nothing rather than an error, and nothing in the published data says which rule produced a row, as osn_tracks has no version column. Consumers would have to be told.
  • The two fixes needed first. Level detection must not pair a start and an end across a hole. Cumulative distance must carry across one. Both are detector changes with their own version, and neither is designed yet. After them, the event comparison should be run again.
  • Whether climbing edges stay on. More checkable holes would firm up or remove the provisional status. Holes from more days are the way to get them.
  • Whether descending edges should be tried again on a larger sample, once the timing of the near-edge cases is understood.
  • What a lower floor gives now. 10,000 ft has not been run with altitude read past blanks.
  • Whether to add an airport test, asking if any airport the aircraft could have stopped at fits inside the hole. Not built here, because it needs a join outside the rule's current form. Our literature review calls it the most promising and least proven idea. Whether it is needed depends on the unknown holes above.

Reproducing this

Run from the opdi repository. run_track_link_day.py and the regeneration need the cluster, S3 and Network Manager truth. The pipeline day's tables go under s3a://eurocontrol/opdi-world/research/a10/.

export PYSPARK_DRIVER_PYTHON=$PWD/.venv310/bin/python
unset PYSPARK_PYTHON
python benchmarks/run_track_link_day.py --day 2026-06-14 --arm debounced --steps 01 02 02a 03 04 04b
python benchmarks/run_track_link_day.py --day 2026-06-14 --arm cruise_linked --steps 02 02a 03 04 04b
python benchmarks/regenerate_track_v4.py --check     # what is stale; no cluster needed
python benchmarks/regenerate_track_v4.py --force     # final_grid and final_edges over two days, and the pipeline scoring
python benchmarks/build_track_v4_paper.py            # fills this report; no cluster needed

Data. All in papers/track-construction-v4/data/. final_grid/ has the settings search and the headline, final_edges/ the four combinations of climbing and descending edges at the chosen settings, stage2/ the pipeline day. round1, round2, round3_fl250, round3_fl180, round3_fl100 and round4_fl180 are earlier runs, kept to explain the method. They came from earlier code and are not regenerated, and all of them predate reading altitude past blanks, so they are not comparable with the final figures except where this report says so.

Provenance. Each file's script, arguments and commit are in the _manifest.json beside it. The final files record commit 4e4ceeb. They came from regenerate_track_v4.py --force on that commit, and regenerate_track_v4.py --check reports all three jobs as current. The pipeline day's temporary tables (state vectors and raw tracks under research/a10) were deleted afterwards to return bucket space, so re-scoring the pipeline day needs run_track_link_day.py run again first.

Render. index.qmd holds this text with literal numbers and the regeneration step, but has not been rendered, because Quarto and R are not available on the machine that built it. index.html and track-construction-v4.pdf are built from the same text and CSVs by benchmarks/build_track_v4_paper.py (the PDF with --pdf, using reportlab), not through Quarto.
